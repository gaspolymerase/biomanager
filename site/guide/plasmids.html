Your databases

Plasmids

Plasmid records with their sequence and map, and the box each tube is in.

  • At the top, tabs for the databases that go with plasmids — Primers, Glycerol stocks, Viruses — in the order the work runs. Each is an ordinary database with its own sheet (see them); they share one place in the sidebar because each names the plasmid it came from.

The plasmid sheet

  • Show them as a Table, by storage Boxes, or as a List; chips for Mine, Has sequence, In a box.
  • Sequence, beside each plasmid's number in the table, opens its sequence and map (Add sequence when it has none yet); the number opens the same page.
  • New plasmid takes a name, backbone, insert, resistance, owner and a box position (blank takes the next free one) — or paste a list of names to make several. Drop in a SnapGene .dna, GenBank, FASTA or plain-text sequence: its name, features and every annotation note come with it, and a file of several sequences keeps the first and says so.

The sequence and its map

  • Open a plasmid to see its sequence and features on an interactive map you can edit (edits save themselves), full screen if you like. Download above the map gives it as GenBank (with every feature) or FASTA. Versions, below the map, lists every earlier sequence (replaced, cleared or edited) with Restore. Storage and Properties are the other tabs. On Properties, Made from records its backbone, insert or template (a plasmid in the lab, by number or name, or one from outside it such as Addgene #11150) and how it was made; the page then shows Made from and Used to make above the map, and a Family tree both ways. Storage also lists its glycerol stocks, with the box each is in, and the viruses made from it (Viruses). Its address carries its number: plasmid #12 is …/plasmid/12.

Knowing what a sequence holds

  • The Feature library (a link at the top of Plasmids) keeps the lab's elements by sequence: promoters, ITRs and LTRs, WPRE, resistance genes, tags. Add to library, above a well-annotated map (a SnapGene or Addgene file, say), keeps its named features there; an admin can Collect from every plasmid. On the library page, Read elements from files takes them straight from annotated files — the GenBank a repository gives you, a SnapGene .dna, a vendor's map — several at once, without making a plasmid for each. Nothing is built in: every element is one your own files carry, because a sequence typed from memory and wrong by one base would mislabel every map in the lab. An admin can also fetch the common-features pack — about 1,900 elements curated from some 2,000 widely used plasmids, downloaded from the open research archive when you ask for it (about a minute; the page shows it when it is in). Its authors built it by exporting a commercial program's published library, so check anything you rely on; elements you added yourself always win over it. Search the library by name or by sequence. On any other plasmid, Detect features marks each library element wherever it is, on either strand and across the origin, and keeps the map as it was in Versions.
  • If your lab runs a server, whoever looks after it can install pLannotate (a separate, free program) and Annotate with pLannotate then appears above the map. It finds elements by comparing sequences rather than matching them exactly, so it catches a codon-optimised gene or a promoter a base or two off, which the library on its own will miss. Everything it marks is kept in Versions, so it can be undone.

Building a new plasmid

  • Assemble a plasmid, from the top of Plasmids or from above any map, builds the next plasmid out of the ones you have. Fill the tray with fragments — a whole plasmid, one of its features, a region between two bases, or a piece an enzyme leaves — in the order they go together, turning any of them round, and put them together three ways: digest and ligate (cut with the same enzymes and let the sticky ends meet), Gibson / NEBuilder HiFi (neighbouring fragments share twenty-odd bases where they meet; the wizard designs the primers that add them, each with its melting temperature) or Golden Gate (BsaI, BsmBI or BbsI, which cut outside their own site, so every junction is a four-base overhang you chose). When you cut a plasmid, only the enzymes that actually have a site in it are offered, with how many sites each has and the single cutters first, and the piece you pick is drawn on a ring of the plasmid it came off, so you can see which part comes out before you take it. Each junction shows its own bases, and one line tells you what is wrong and which end it is — Fragment 2's 3′ end has no overlap with fragment 3 — rather than that it did not work. A Golden Gate is refused when two junctions take the same overhang, when one is another backwards, or when one reads the same on both strands, because each of those puts the pieces together in more than one way. You see the product's map, its length and every fragment in it before anything is made. Create the plasmid then makes an ordinary plasmid: the features of each fragment at their new places (a feature a cut runs through keeps the part that is there, and says so), each fragment marked on the map so you can see the joins, Made from filled in for every plasmid it came out of with the enzymes and coordinates used, the first entry in its Versions, and the library's elements marked if you asked. A Gibson's primers are saved in the Primers database marked to order and drawn on the map of the plasmid each one amplifies, so Copy for ordering picks them up with the rest.
  • A primer you draw on the map (select bases, then Edit → Create → Primer) is also kept in the lab's Primers database, marked to order, with the plasmid in its Plasmid column. The Primers card under the map lists every primer for this plasmid, where it binds and any 5′ tail; tick some, then Copy for ordering (name and sequence, one per line, to paste into a supplier's form) or Export for ordering (a CSV).

Tubes, boxes and files

  • Files, on Storage, keeps what belongs with the plasmid: the sequencing reads (.ab1) that confirmed it, gel photos, the vendor's datasheet. Say in one line what they show. Pictures show a thumbnail; whoever added a file, or an admin, can take it off again. They come with your data in Export my data.
  • Each tube keeps its miniprep's Concentration (ng/µL) and 260/280, in the sheet and on its Storage tab, which also lists the notebook pages that link it.

Sharing and bulk changes

  • Belongs to: Personal, or Lab common — anyone can edit it (a shared stock plasmid), while only its owner or an admin changes whose it is or deletes it. The Lab common chip lists them.
  • Select plasmids to set owner, resistance or Belongs to, move them to a box, or delete. Moved into another box with no position, a plasmid takes its next free cell. Import from Excel brings in your old list.
